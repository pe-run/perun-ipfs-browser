The internet was meant to be free, open, and decentralized. What happened? Governments don't like it when they can't control us. That makes it harder to find hardware and software that respect our privacy and don't track our every move. Big corporations also want control over their users, their data, and the services they depend on. When a new tool gives people more independence, sooner or later it disappears or gets pushed aside (under pressure from Big Brother?).

Skype is a good example. It started as a groundbreaking messenger that used peer-to-peer technology to connect users directly. Over time it became a centralized service that depended on one company's infrastructure. In 2025, Microsoft shut down the consumer version of Skype.

Some browsers that introduced decentralized technologies followed a similar path. Opera began work on IPFS integration in 2019. In 2020 it made sites using IPFS available on Android. In 2021 it expanded that support to desktop, mainly through HTTP gateways. Beaker Browser used Dat and Hypercore to let people publish and browse sites directly over a peer-to-peer network. The project was archived in 2022. Development of Netbox.Browser, another attempt at decentralized browsing, was frozen that same year. In 2021, Brave integrated a local IPFS node into the browser. Users could access the network without additional software or public gateways. Brave removed the feature in August 2024, citing low adoption and maintenance costs. Users lost a convenient way to access IPFS directly.

A similar dependency exists with public IPFS gateways, which let regular browsers open decentralized resources. Cloudflare launched its own gateway in 2018 and began redirecting its traffic to the IPFS Foundation's gateways in 2024. In 2026 the w3s.link gateway shut down, and ipfs.io and dweb.link replaced their traditional gateways with a tool that lets the browser fetch and verify content directly from the peer-to-peer network. Access through a traditional gateway depends on its operator. When it shuts down, links that point through it may stop working even while the data still exists on IPFS.

You can install and configure IPFS software yourself, but many people find that too complicated or time-consuming. The decentralized internet is hard to bring to a wider audience when access requires technical knowledge, extra software, and manual setup. That's where the idea for Perun IPFS Browser came from. It supports IPFS natively and puts decentralization, independence, and user freedom first.

Perun IPFS Browser opens IPFS and IPNS resources as easily as ordinary web pages. Enter the address in the browser bar. You don't need to install a separate node, configure a gateway, or know the details of the protocol. Perun connects directly to the IPFS network and also supports traditional HTTP and HTTPS sites. You can browse both parts of the internet in the same program.

Speed matters to us. In our tests so far, Perun opened IPFS resources faster than the other browsers we compared it with. We focused on efficient downloads and fast page loading. For IPFS to catch on beyond a circle of enthusiasts, it has to be easy and fast to use every day.

Perun lets you pin the content you care about and share it with others. A pinned page, document, or photo can stay available even if the original publisher shuts down their server, stops paying for hosting, or loses their account with a service provider. Data can stay available practically indefinitely, as long as at least one reachable device stores and shares it. Anyone can help preserve a part of the internet that matters to them.

As Perun develops, we want to use peer-to-peer networking for direct communication and information sharing. Imagine communities where people can publish content, have conversations, and share files without relying on central servers run by one company. Participants build that network, share its resources, and help each other keep information available. That's what we're working toward.

Decentralization has its limits. Content still needs to be stored somewhere, and finding and sharing it depends on which peers are available. Shutting down one server, however, doesn't erase independently stored copies. No single company runs the entire protocol, and there's no single switch that can shut down the whole community. Everyone who shares resources can help build independent internet infrastructure.

We currently develop Perun IPFS Browser for Linux. Versions for other operating systems are in preparation. We want everyone to be able to use the decentralized internet without technical expertise. IPFS support should be a core browser feature. We're building around open protocols so users can preserve content that matters to them and communicate without unnecessary intermediaries.

The history of Skype, browsers dropping peer-to-peer support, and IPFS gateways closing shows how easily we can lose access to tools we rely on. The future of the internet shouldn't depend solely on decisions made by governments and large corporations. We need technology that gives users a choice, helps them stay independent, and makes access to information harder to control centrally. Perun IPFS Browser is our step in that direction.

The internet belongs to everyone. We shouldn't let a few corporations decide what we can view, publish, or store. Anyone who uses a decentralized network can help it grow, preserve important content, and make the internet more resilient to censorship, outages, and arbitrary decisions by individual providers.
