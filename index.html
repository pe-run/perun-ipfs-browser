The internet was supposed to be free, open, and decentralized. What happened to it? Governments don't like it when they can't control us. That is why it is getting harder to find hardware and software that truly respect our privacy and do not track our every move. Large corporations also prefer to keep users under their control, collect their data, and make them dependent on their own services. Whenever an interesting solution gives people more independence, sooner or later it disappears or gets pushed to the margins (under pressure from Big Brother?).

Skype is a good example. It started out as a revolutionary messaging app using peer-to-peer technology that allowed users to communicate directly. Over time, it was turned into a centralized service, and in 2025 it was shut down completely. A technology that gave users greater independence was replaced with a solution that depended on one company's infrastructure, and then simply ceased to exist.

A similar story can be seen with web browsers that tried to introduce decentralized technologies. Opera began work on IPFS integration in 2019, introduced Android support in 2020, and in 2021 made it possible to open IPFS addresses on desktop as well, though mainly through HTTP gateways. Beaker Browser (2017-2022) let users publish and browse websites directly over a peer-to-peer network, using Dat and Hypercore instead of IPFS, but development was discontinued. Netbox.Browser (2019-2022) also pursued decentralized web browsing, but development and support for the project were frozen in 2022. Brave (2021-2024) went a step further by integrating an actual IPFS node directly into the browser. This was a revolutionary solution that made it possible to use the decentralized internet without installing additional software or relying on public gateways. Unfortunately, the feature was removed in August 2024. Was this purely a business and technical decision, or did reluctance toward technologies that make the internet harder to control centrally play a role as well? Whatever the reasons, the result was the same: users lost convenient access to IPFS.

The problem extends to public IPFS gateways, which allow users to open decentralized resources in ordinary browsers. Cloudflare, which had been providing such a gateway since 2018, began shifting its traffic to other providers in 2024. In 2026, well-known public gateways such as ipfs.io, dweb.link, and w3s.link also ceased operating. This exposes a fundamental weakness: using a decentralized network through a centralized gateway makes you dependent on an outside provider again. When a gateway shuts down, users lose access to resources through it even though the data itself may still exist on the IPFS network.

Of course, users can still install and configure IPFS software themselves, but most either don't know how or simply don't want to spend the time. The decentralized internet won't become widely used if using it requires specialist knowledge, installing additional software, and setting it up by hand. That is where the idea for Perun IPFS Browser came from, a browser with native IPFS support that puts decentralization, independence, and user freedom first.

Perun IPFS Browser makes using IPFS and IPNS resources as natural as visiting ordinary websites. There is no need to install a separate IPFS node, configure public gateways, or know how the protocol works in detail. The browser was designed to access the decentralized network directly, without relying on public HTTP gateways as intermediaries. It also supports traditional HTTP and HTTPS sites, so it can serve as an everyday web browser. You don't have to choose between the traditional internet and the decentralized internet. You can use both in the same program.

Speed is one of the project's most important goals. In our experience, Perun is currently the fastest browser for accessing IPFS resources. We have focused on efficient data retrieval and on opening decentralized pages as quickly as possible. We believe IPFS should be more than a curiosity for technology enthusiasts. To become a real alternative to the traditional internet, it needs to be easy and fast to use above all.

Perun also lets you pin the content you care about, so you can take part in storing it and sharing it with other users. A pinned page, document, photo, or other resource no longer has to depend on the original publisher's server. Data can remain available on the network practically indefinitely as long as at least one reachable device keeps storing and sharing it. That means valuable content doesn't have to disappear just because a site owner shut down the server, stopped paying for hosting, or a service provider chose to delete the account. Every user can help preserve a part of the internet that they consider important.

This is only the beginning of what peer-to-peer networking makes possible. Perun is meant to be both a browser for decentralized sites and a gateway to direct communication and information exchange between users. Imagine an internet where people can form communities, publish content, hold discussions, and share files without having to rely on central servers controlled by a single company. An internet where users build the network themselves, share its resources, and help one another keep information available. These features are part of the direction we are taking with Perun's future development.

Decentralization does not automatically solve all of the internet's problems. Content still has to be stored somewhere, and finding and sharing it depends on which participants are available. Yet there is no single central server whose shutdown would necessarily make every independently stored copy disappear. No one company manages the entire protocol, and no single switch can shut down the whole community. The more people use these tools and share resources, the greater the opportunities to build independent internet infrastructure.

Perun IPFS Browser is currently being developed for Linux, with versions for other operating systems in preparation. The project is under active development. Its goal is to make the decentralized internet accessible to everyone, including people who are neither programmers nor network specialists. We want IPFS support to be a core browser feature, beyond the status of an experimental add-on that can be removed in the next update. We have no intention of building another closed platform that makes users dependent on one provider. We want to develop a tool that lets people use open protocols, preserve the content that matters to them, and communicate without unnecessary intermediaries.

The history of Skype, browsers that dropped peer-to-peer support, and IPFS gateways that shut down shows how easily we can lose access to tools we used to rely on. The future of the internet should therefore not depend entirely on the decisions of governments and large corporations. We need technologies that give users a real choice, let them stay independent, and make it harder to control access to information centrally. Perun IPFS Browser is our step in that direction.

The internet belongs to everyone. Let's keep it from becoming the property of a handful of corporations that can decide at any moment what we're allowed to view, publish, and store. Everyone who uses a decentralized network can help it grow, help preserve important content, and make the internet more resilient to censorship, outages, and arbitrary decisions by individual providers.

Explore the decentralized internet. Keep what matters. Communicate without unnecessary intermediaries. Use the web on your own terms.
